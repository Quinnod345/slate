# The treatment

A treatment is the film on a page or two, before the script: what it's really about, how it feels, and where its weight
falls. It's what the owner approves in spirit before approving words, and what builders read to know why each act
exists. Write one for anything longer than a short ad; for a reel or an ad, its first four sections are enough.

## Template

1. **The idea, as a reframe.** One or two sentences that say what the film is really about, not what it lists. A good
   reframe changes the question ("a score is a verdict until someone reads it with you").
2. **Runtime rationale.** Why this length and not shorter or longer. Tie it to the format and to what the viewer needs
   to feel or do by the end.
3. **Act by act.** For each act: its job in one line, what the viewer learns, what they feel, and how it hands to the
   next.
4. **The emotional arc.** A small table: act, where the viewer starts, where they end.
5. **Memorable moments.** One signature moment per act is the starting recipe; name the peaks, sustained passage or
   stillness that this brief actually needs (signature-moments.md).
6. **The character's gesture vocabulary** (character-led films only): the few gestures it has, each with one meaning
   (slate-motion).
7. **Voice direction.** Who's speaking, how they sound, what they never sound like (slate-voice).
8. **Look and sound.** A line each, using the current direction and the applicable brand approvals. Record requested
   revisions alongside the earlier look and palette, so a creative change does not erase its history.
9. **What the film never shows.** Features ruled out, words the brand avoids, claims not made. Builders and checks read
   this list.
10. **The owner's calls.** Decisions already made, in their words.
11. **Notes for builders.** Anything a builder needs that isn't in the script: a recurring object, a shared surface,
    where the camera rests.

## Craft

- **Reframe before you write.** State the request as a job to be done, then ask what the real problem behind it is. The
  strongest films solve a better problem than the one stated.
- **The visionary bar.** Give the intended moments enough force to make someone cry, inside the chosen visual
  language. Their number comes from the treatment, not a universal quota. Hero moments are reviewed against the bar.
- **Clear attention per beat.** Earlier films used one carrier per act and one object per phrase. A different brief
  can use parallel action when viewers can still follow the intended idea.
- **Show, then say less.** Keywords were the product-film starting recipe. A transcript or another text treatment can
  serve a different brief when its configured reading policy and actual screen time support it.
- **Name the payoff early.** If the film ends on something, set it up visibly in the first act (structure.md).
- **Keep the baseline and the requested change.** A derived or re-cut film starts from the earlier film and records
  what changes. The latest authorized feedback governs the revision; do not turn a prior approval into a ban on the
  requested redesign or ask for that same creative choice again.

## Handing over

The treatment goes to the pre-production sitting with the script, or earlier on its own if the idea itself needs a yes.
Record the owner's calls on it as numbered decisions in the tracker. When the script contradicts the treatment later,
change one of them on purpose and say so; don't let them drift.
