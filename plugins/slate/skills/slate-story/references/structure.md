# Story structure: shapes that work

Patterns that held up across three films, and the rules behind them. Use them as starting points, never as a template to
fill in regardless of the brief.

## Openings and endings

- **Bookend the film.** The close can play the opening's construction in reverse, or return to the composition the film
  opened on, with the call to action landing where the opening's first word landed.
- **The end card carries the call to action in text** as well as in the voice, and holds long enough to read twice.
- **Open out of silence** and let the first image establish the world before the first word.

## Payoffs

- **Set up a payoff visibly.** Show what's missing now (an empty dashed slot beside the thing nobody measures) and fill
  it later in the film. The viewer remembers the gap.
- **The product's own empty state** (a placeholder before the first reading) is a truthful setup for a reveal that pays
  it off.
- **Answer the viewer's "what does it do for me?"** in one object they can hold, with a page for every door it opens.
- **Leave a decision visibly to the viewer**: the last box stays empty, and nobody on screen touches it.

## Making a process into a story

- Turn a product's steps into a story about one person (the sample user decides, commits, discovers), so a process
  reads as a journey rather than a feature list.
- Follow the product's real journey order; a film that jumps around the product teaches the wrong model of it.
- Stage only what the voice names: three spoken items get three taps, not twelve.

## Remaking or answering an earlier film

- When a film is modelled on an original, follow it topic for topic, then rebuild every graphic in the current craft:
  the original's content in the new film's look.
- Recover the original's exact on-screen content by grabbing its frames (`slate analyze <video>`), not from memory or
  summaries.
- Check every claim in the original against the current product and site; the new film carries only what's still true
  (truth.md).

## Acts

- Every act has one job, one carrier (the object it's about) and one signature moment.
- An act break with no voice for a couple of seconds is a natural breath: the score can open there.
- Hand acts to each other on carries (an object that continues across), never on a cut to a title card.

## Words on screen and words in the voice

- On-screen text is keywords, not a transcript: about 40% of the words the voice says in that shot, or fewer.
- When the script's picture notes and its on-screen column disagree on wording, the on-screen column's words go on
  screen.
- The on-screen word budget (12 per shot) can justify leaving product elements out of a shot; record the omission in the
  act's notes.
